The map says who runs out of runway. This says what buys it back.

Runway is the number of months a San Diego ZIP has before median rent crosses half of median income. Pull the three levers below and every ZIP’s runway recomputes against the same formula the map uses.

Runway today vs. under each lever

How the runway is computed

Must match the map's definition — confirm before the joint rehearsal.

Model

burden(t) = burden₀ × [ (1 + rent_growth) / (1 + income_growth) ] t / 12
runway = smallest t where burden(t) ≥ 0.50

How each lever enters

  • Minimum wage divides today’s burden by (1 + increase). Growth rates untouched — a level shift, not a trend change.
  • Rent stabilization replaces rent_growth with min(rent_growth, cap). It bends the curve rather than shifting it, so it does nothing for a ZIP already past 50%.
  • Housing voucher subtracts a share of max(burden − 0.30, 0) from today’s burden — the only lever that can pull an already-burdened ZIP back below the line.

Reading the chart

A ZIP already at or past 50% burden has zero runway and shows a red tick at the axis origin. Bars are clipped at 120 months; a hatched end means the ZIP does not cross 50% within ten years under that lever. Where income growth matches or beats rent growth, burden never crosses at all — those read as 10 yr+.

Known gaps

Growth rates are held constant, so this is a trend projection and not a forecast — no supply response, no induced rent increases from voucher uptake, no migration. The voucher lever in particular models the household’s out-of-pocket burden, not the market rent, so it understates any landlord-side price effect. Worth saying out loud to judges rather than letting them find it.
