Four in nine San Diego households are in the red.

Each of the 1,171,123 synthetic households in the county's Household Living Budget model carries an income and a budget — what it costs to meet a basic-needs standard where they live. 520,257 earn less than their budget requires. Three independent measures put the county within a point of that figure. This page asks what it would take to change it.

What a practical package would take

A targeted package combines a 25% income lift, a rent subsidy limiting the household payment to 30% of income for households under half the county median, and coverage of half of childcare costs. Public outlay and employer-paid wages are kept separate; neither is presented as a forecast or funding proposal.

Households still short

Housing is the largest single cost, but it is not the whole shortfall. Even a universal 30%-of-income rent cap by itself moves only of households currently below their budget. The model therefore shows the package and its limit instead of promising one lever can solve the problem.

San Diego County's own Health & Human Services self-sufficiency budget for a family of four is $8,155 a month. This model puts the mean required income for a household in the red at $8,701 — a 7% gap between two independently built budgets.

Method & limits

These are synthetic households, not real ones. The population is built by fitting American Community Survey tract totals and sampling real PUMS households as donors, so it is valid for distributions and geographic patterns — never for claims about any individual household. Group-quarters residents (military barracks, nursing homes, student housing, correctional facilities) are excluded entirely, which is a real omission in this county.

The threshold

A household is in the red when its gross annual income is below hlb_year: the cost of a basic-needs budget plus the tax owed at that income. Both sides are gross 2024 dollars, so it is a like-for-like comparison. This is the organizers' own published measure, not one we invented.

Housing is required rent, not rent paid

housing_cost_month is the HUD FY2024 Small Area Fair Market Rent for the tract at the bedroom count the household's size implies. It is a normative standard, not observed spending, and it is not tenure-adjusted — an owner with a paid-off mortgage is still assigned a market rent. Read it as "what housing costs here," not "what this household pays."

How the levers enter

The raise multiplies income. The rent subsidy replaces what the household pays with min(FMR, cap × income). The childcare subsidy reduces childcare cost. "Other essentials" does not move when a subsidy fires: the organizers define it as 20% of food plus housing, where housing is the cost of the unit — and a subsidy changes who pays that rent, not what the unit costs. Letting it fall had been overstating the rent lever by 2.0 points.

What we could not model

No supply response. A subsidy at this scale would push market rents up, eating some of the gain shown here. Childcare is charged whenever children under 12 are present, whether or not an adult works. Tenure is not in the data, so an owner who has paid off a mortgage is charged a market rent they do not pay — the ACS row in the table above is the closest available check on how much that matters. Rents are FY2024, themselves trended from a late-2021 survey; re-priced to FY2026 the county median rises about 8%. Treat all of this as the arithmetic of a policy, not a forecast of one.

The tax term

When a lever changes the budget, the tax owed on it changes too. Within a composition type that tax is a deterministic function of the budget — among households sharing a type and a budget the published figures differ by one cent — so we read the changed budget off each type's own observed curve, and never extrapolate beyond it. At baseline it returns the published tax exactly. The previous approach scaled tax by a fixed per-household ratio, which understated the rent lever by 1.5 points; that error and the "other essentials" error above pointed in opposite directions and partly cancelled, so correcting only one would have been worse than correcting neither.

Not San Diego's voucher

The rent lever is voucher-shaped, not a model of the program that exists. It uses gross rather than adjusted income, and it is offered to every eligible household. In the real city, the Housing Commission is a Moving to Work agency: work-able households pay the greater of 40% of adjusted income or a minimum contribution. Both county waiting lists are closed — one with 82,848 applicants and nobody selected since August 2022 — and roughly 1.3% of county households are assisted. Read the lever as "what a subsidy of this shape would do", never as "what vouchers would do".

Verification

Our reconstruction of the threshold reproduces the organizers' economically_vulnerable flag for 1,171,122 of 1,171,123 households. The build refuses to ship if that count grows, if any grid cell disagrees with a fresh computation, if a slider's range no longer matches the grid, or if the balance sheet above does not add up. The one exception sits nine cents from its threshold, where the published cost columns are rounded to cents but the budget total is not.

Tracts

732 tracts in the release; 5 are excluded for holding fewer than 100 households, as the data dictionary advises before ranking or mapping. Two more — 06073009510 and 06073009511 — are kept in every total but left out of the featured table. They are the only two of 727 where most households have children under 12, with uniformly maxed bedroom counts and low recorded income: the signature of military family housing, where the housing allowance is excluded from recorded income while this model still charges full market rent. We cannot confirm that from the release, so we disclose it rather than correct it.
